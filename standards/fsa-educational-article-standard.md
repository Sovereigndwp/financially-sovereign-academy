# FSA Educational Article Standard

This is the master standard for the FSA Money Ideas article library. It defines what a Money Ideas article is, who it serves, how it should sound, how it must be structured, and the editorial rules that govern it from first draft to public publication. Anyone writing, reviewing, or approving an article for Financially Sovereign Academy should read this document first and treat it as the reference of record. When another FSA document conflicts with this one, this standard governs unless a newer version explicitly supersedes it.

## Purpose and positioning

FSA Money Ideas is a library of short articles that teach the idea beneath a money topic. Each article runs about four to eight minutes of reading. The goal is understanding, not instruction. A Money Ideas article explains why something works the way it does, so that a reader can reason about their own situation with more clarity later. It is not a list of tips, not a set of rules to follow, and not advice.

The distinction matters. Tips tell someone what to do. Money Ideas explains how a piece of the money world actually behaves, names the misconception that usually gets in the way, and gives the reader a reusable mental model. A person who finishes a good article should be able to look at a bill, an offer, a fee, or a decision and see the underlying structure more clearly. That is the product.

FSA Money Ideas is free, generous public education. There are no paywalls, no upsells, and no intake funnels inside the article library. Specialized help is offered only when a situation becomes genuinely personal and the reader asks for it, and even then it is offered as a next step, not sold inside the lesson. The library earns trust by being useful without asking for anything back.

These articles are also explicitly not financial, legal, tax, or investment advice. They explain options and tradeoffs. They never tell an individual what to do with their own money, their own case, or their own filing. The single education-only notice in the footer carries that boundary so the articles themselves can stay focused on teaching.

## Audience

FSA writes for a wide and mixed readership, and the standard assumes that range at all times. The primary readers are individual learners trying to understand money on their own terms. Around them sit several other groups that shape how an article must be written.

Educators and facilitators use these articles in classrooms, workshops, and one-on-one sessions, often printing them. Workforce and job-readiness programs use them with adults who are rebuilding stability. Correctional and reentry programs use them under real constraints: limited or no internet, no smartphone during the day, no active bank account, and printed materials only. Community organizations, coaches, and nonprofit facilitators hand these articles to people in moments of stress. And a large share of direct readers arrive on mobile phones, on slow or metered connections, sometimes reading in a second language.

Every article must work for all of these at once. That means the writing has to be clear on a small screen, complete on a printed page, respectful of someone reading it in a hard week, and safe to use in a setting where the reader cannot or should not go online, log into an account, or reveal personal financial details. When a design or exercise choice would serve one audience but exclude another, the standard favors the more constrained reader.

## The FSA voice

The FSA voice is clear, thoughtful, grounded, calm, observant, practical, respectful, and intellectually serious. It sounds like a knowledgeable person explaining something carefully to an equal. It takes the reader seriously as a thinker.

It is not preachy, childish, academic, bureaucratic, promotional, artificially inspirational, or template-like. It does not lecture, does not talk down, does not perform enthusiasm, and does not hide plain ideas behind jargon. Use contractions naturally. Vary sentence length so the prose has rhythm. Prefer paragraphs to lists, and reach for a list only when a checklist or a genuinely enumerated set helps the reader more than prose would.

Respect for the reader is a hard rule, not a tone preference. Never imply that financial difficulty proves laziness, ignorance, irresponsibility, lack of discipline, or moral failure. Money outcomes are shaped by forces the reader did not choose. Acknowledge those forces honestly: low or unstable income, caregiving responsibilities, high housing costs, limited banking access, emergencies, discrimination, poorly designed products, information gaps, behavioral pressures, and institutional constraints. Acknowledging these does not remove the reader's agency, and the writing should hold both truths at once. A person can face real barriers and still make meaningful choices. The voice honors both.

### Banned phrases

The following phrases are not allowed anywhere in an article, because each one signals filler, hype, or template writing:

- "In today's fast-paced world"
- "Let's dive in"
- "The bottom line"
- "It is important to note"
- "Whether you are a beginner or an expert"
- "Empower your financial journey"
- "Unlock"
- "Navigate the complexities"

Beyond the named list, avoid the patterns they represent: generic motivational endings, chains of rhetorical questions, strings of one-sentence paragraphs used for drama, decorative emoji, and em dashes. Use commas, colons, or periods where an em dash would tempt you. American English throughout.

## The required 13-part structure

Every Money Ideas article follows the same thirteen-part structure, in order. The structure is what makes the library coherent and what lets a reader trust the shape of any article they open. Each part has a job. Below is what each part is for and what good looks like.

**1. Title.** A natural title, the way a thoughtful person would name the idea in conversation. Not a textbook heading, not a keyword string, not a promise of a secret. Good titles name the tension or the idea plainly and make a curious reader want the explanation.

**2. Opening tension.** Open with a situation, a contradiction, or a question. Never open with a definition. The reader should feel a small pull, a sense that something here does not quite add up or matters more than it first appears. This is where the article earns the next two minutes.

**3. The common belief.** State the misconception plainly, the belief most people hold about this topic. Name it fairly, in the words a reasonable person would use, without ridicule. The reader should recognize the belief, and possibly recognize themselves in it.

**4. What is actually happening.** Explain, in plain language, how the thing really works. This is the core teaching move. No jargon before it is explained. Keep the explanation concrete and mechanical enough that the reader can picture it.

**5. A concrete scenario.** Give one realistic scenario drawn from ordinary life: work, bills, family, transportation, banking, credit, food, housing, or an unexpected expense. Keep the numbers simple and clean so the reader can follow the arithmetic in their head or on paper. The scenario is an illustration, and it should feel like a real week in a real life.

**6. The mental model.** Distill the lesson into one short, reusable idea the reader can carry. A good mental model is a single sentence or a compact image that the reader could repeat to a friend a week later. This is the thing they keep.

**7. A second scenario or boundary case.** Show where the idea changes with circumstances. Avoid presenting one universally correct choice. Include at least one case where the obvious recommendation may not be the right one, because circumstances flip it. This part is what keeps the article honest and prevents it from becoming a disguised tip.

**8. Why this matters.** Explain why understanding this idea changes how the reader sees their money, without inflating the stakes and without a motivational flourish. Say plainly what the idea protects the reader from, or what it lets them notice.

**9. Look for this today.** Give one observation exercise the reader can do in ordinary life today. Not a task with an account, just something to notice: a fee on a receipt, a term on a bill, a pattern in a store. Observation builds the habit of seeing the idea in the wild.

**10. Try it yourself.** Give one short reflection, comparison, or calculation. It must work with pencil and paper and require no account, no app, and no internet. Keep the math simple and the instructions unambiguous, because a facilitator may hand this to a room.

**11. One question to carry with you.** End the body with one open question. It should be genuinely open, not moralizing, and not a rhetorical setup for an answer the article already gave. The question invites the reader to keep thinking.

**12. Related learning.** Point the reader onward: related articles, related FSA modules by their exact canonical names, a relevant tool if one applies, and a suggested next article. This is where the library connects to itself.

**13. Sources and review note.** List sources for any current factual claims, with dates, and note anything marked for review. This part is where the article shows its work and stays accountable.

## The misconception method

Every article names exactly one misconception and treats it fairly. One, not several, so the article stays focused. Fairness is the method: the reader should feel understood, not corrected.

Work the misconception through six moves, woven into the relevant parts of the structure rather than listed mechanically. First, explain why the belief seems reasonable, because it usually is reasonable given how things look from the outside. Second, say what the belief gets right, since most misconceptions contain a real observation. Third, show what it misses, the piece of the mechanism that the belief overlooks. Fourth, offer a better model that accounts for what the belief missed. Fifth, say when the better model is genuinely useful, so it does not become a new rigid rule. Sixth, admit where even the better model has limits, because no single idea covers every case. An article that skips the fairness and jumps straight to correction has failed the method even if its facts are right.

## Length and reading level

The default length is 900 to 1,400 words. A narrow concept that does not need the full range may run 650 to 900 words. Do not pad an article to reach a number, and do not compress a genuinely rich idea below the point of clarity.

Write at a grades 7 to 9 reading level in plain adult language. Plain does not mean simplistic. It means short common words where they work, clear sentences, and no unexplained jargon. Always explain the idea before naming the technical term for it. Once you have named a term, use it consistently for the rest of the article rather than switching synonyms, which confuses readers who are new to the vocabulary.

## Scenario standards

Scenarios are the heart of a Money Ideas article, and they carry real responsibility. Every scenario must be plausible, numerically simple, and culturally respectful. Every scenario must be original to FSA, invented for the article, and never lifted from another lesson or a real person's disclosed circumstances.

Keep numbers round and easy to follow, and state your assumptions openly so the reader can see the setup. Avoid false precision: do not attach exact figures to things that vary widely, and do not imply a scenario is data. A scenario is an illustration and should read as one. Never present a scenario as evidence for a claim.

Avoid stereotypes of any kind, including stereotypes about who struggles with money, what kinds of jobs people hold, what neighborhoods or families look like, or how people in a given country or community behave. Draw scenarios from the ordinary texture of working life across many situations. And include, across the article, at least one case where the obvious recommendation may not be the right one, so the reader learns to weigh circumstances rather than follow a script.

## Evidence standards

FSA sorts evidence into three tiers, and the writing must respect the difference.

Tier A is stable concepts: scarcity, opportunity cost, present bias, compounding, liquidity, incentives, and similar durable ideas. These may rest on established academic or institutional sources and do not need fresh dating, because they do not change year to year.

Tier B is current factual claims: fees, interest rates, laws, credit-score models, program rules, account-protection limits, tax thresholds, inflation figures, and anything else that shifts over time. Every Tier B claim must be researched at the time of writing and dated in the article. If a Tier B fact cannot be verified confidently, say so plainly and mark it for review rather than stating it as settled.

Tier C is illustrative material that FSA creates, mainly scenarios. It must be labeled as illustration and never presented as evidence.

Never invent statistics, laws, study findings, quotes, product features, historical facts, or survey results. When the evidence is uncertain, say so in the text and flag it in the review note. It is always better to admit a gap than to manufacture a fact.

### Preferred source order

When sourcing a factual claim, prefer sources in this order:

1. Government agencies
2. Central banks, where the topic is monetary and it is appropriate
3. Academic papers
4. Established nonprofit educational institutions
5. Official regulatory materials
6. High-quality research organizations

Cite the underlying primary source, not a secondary explainer. Do not cite another educational article simply because it explains a concept clearly. If that article rests on a government figure or a study, find and cite that figure or study directly.

## Financial neutrality and the education-only notice

FSA gives no individualized investment, legal, tax, credit-repair, debt-settlement, insurance, or product-endorsement advice. Instead of telling a reader what to do, explain the options and the tradeoffs. Useful framings include phrasings such as "One factor to consider is...", "The better choice depends on...", "Before acting, verify the current rules.", and "A qualified professional may be needed when...". The article's job is to make the reader a better reasoner about their own decision, not to make the decision for them.

Do not bury the learning under a pile of disclaimers. One clear education-only notice lives in the standard footer and covers the whole article:

> Created by Dalia · financiallysovereign.academy · Educational content only · Not financial, legal, tax, or investment advice.

That single notice is sufficient. Repeating warnings throughout the body signals anxiety and clutters the teaching. Trust the footer to hold the boundary.

## Bitcoin language rules

FSA is practical financial literacy first. Bitcoin appears only when it genuinely improves the lesson, most naturally in articles about inflation, banking, monetary systems, custody, or sovereignty. Do not insert Bitcoin into an article where it does not help the reader understand the topic at hand.

When Bitcoin does appear, follow these language rules precisely. Capitalize "Bitcoin" when referring to the network, the protocol, or the asset. Use lowercase "bitcoin" for units of the currency. Distinguish Bitcoin from other cryptocurrencies, and never use "crypto" as a substitute word for Bitcoin. Keep several distinctions clear for the reader: self-custody versus custodial ownership, market-price volatility versus protocol risk, saving in Bitcoin versus short-term trading, and Bitcoin as an asset versus Bitcoin as a guaranteed investment. Acknowledge risk honestly. Make no price predictions and no return promises. Bitcoin enters an FSA article as a subject to understand, never as a product to sell.

## Reentry and vulnerable-population rules

When an article will be used in reentry or with other vulnerable readers, additional rules apply, and in practice they improve every article, so treat them as defaults.

Assume limited or no internet. Avoid any exercise that requires an active bank account or a smartphone app. Never ask learners to disclose criminal history, debt balances, trauma, or any personal financial data. Include realistic barriers in scenarios where they belong: missing identification, account screening and denials, unstable or informal employment, housing deposits, scams, layered fees, and limited transportation. Do not promise specific outcomes, because outcomes depend on circumstances the reader does not fully control. Avoid shaming in every form. And make sure the print exercises work fully offline with pencil and paper, because that is how many of these readers will meet the material.

## Youth adaptation rules

When an article will be used with young learners, adapt with care. Do not assume the student has parental financial support, and do not build lessons that depend on it. Do not ask students to reveal household income or any family financial detail. Do not turn a family's financial choices into a classroom spectacle. Use low-stakes examples that a young person can reason about without exposure. Note clearly when legal rules differ for minors, such as rules about accounts, contracts, or work. And keep exercises separated from real transactions, so a lesson never pushes a minor toward an action that should involve a guardian or a legal adult.

## Accessibility and mobile standards

Articles must meet a high accessibility bar, because a large part of the audience depends on it. Use semantic HTML so structure is meaningful to assistive technology. Support full keyboard navigation with a visible focus indicator. Maintain sufficient color contrast, and never convey meaning by color alone. Use responsive type that stays readable on a small screen and a comfortable reading measure on a large one. Write for screen readers with descriptive link text rather than bare "click here" links. Respect reduced-motion preferences. Make touch targets at least 44 pixels so they work on a phone held in one hand.

Mobile is a first-class case, not an afterthought. Many readers arrive on phones over slow or metered connections. Keep pages light, avoid heavy assets, and make sure the article reads well and completely before any optional interactive element loads.

## The print standard

Every article must have a print version that stands on its own, because facilitators and reentry programs depend on paper. The print version is black and white and must not rely on background color to carry meaning. It has to be readable at letter size with clear headings. Where a URL matters, show it visibly, since a printed link cannot be clicked. No interactive element may be required to understand the content. All exercises must work with pencil and paper. Nothing important may be hidden behind hover, since hover does not exist on paper. Keep ink use minimal. Include the education-only notice, and put the article ID and revision date in the footer so a printed copy can be traced to its source and version. A typical print version runs three to five pages.

## Design and brand rules

Articles use the existing FSA visual system through `css/fsa-brand.css` plus `css/fsa-articles.css`. Introduce no new design tokens and no page-local `:root` brand block. Work within the system that already exists. Emerald and mint are accents only, used on neutral-dark surfaces, never as the dominant color of a page. Classes follow the `.fsa-*` and `.mi-*` conventions.

The visual feel is calm, credible, editorial, and spacious. Reach for a visual device only when it genuinely aids understanding: a comparison, a timeline, a decision tree, a two-column tradeoff, a cost-over-time chart, or a small concept diagram. Never use decorative emoji, and never use em dashes in the prose. Visual restraint is part of the brand's credibility.

## Analytics and privacy

Analytics are first-party only, through the existing FSA analytics service. FSA does not use surveillance-style reading analytics, does not track scroll depth or dwell time to profile individual readers, and does not use third-party trackers or ad pixels. The library measures what it needs to improve the content and nothing that would compromise a reader's privacy. This matters especially for the reentry and vulnerable audiences, who have real reasons to distrust being watched. Respecting that is part of the product's integrity, not a compliance checkbox.

## Editorial states and the public-visibility rule

Every article moves through a defined lifecycle, and its state determines whether it is visible to the public. The states are: planned (a pre-draft registry entry), idea, outlined, drafted, fact-checked, educational-review, accessibility-review, approved, published, revision-needed, and archived. The separate reviewStatus field tracks review progress with its own vocabulary: not-started, educational-review-required, human-review-required, in-review, and approved.

The public-visibility rule is strict and non-negotiable. Only articles in the approved or published state render on the public site. Every other state carries a visible status banner and a robots noindex directive, so unfinished work can never be mistaken for finished work by a reader or a search engine.

The writing process itself must never mark its own draft as approved or published. A drafting pass stops at status `drafted` with reviewStatus `human-review-required`, and a human carries it the rest of the way. That separation is what keeps the human review gates meaningful.

## Pre-publish checklist

Before a human moves an article to approved, confirm each of the following:

1. The article follows all thirteen parts in order, and each part does its job.
2. Exactly one misconception is named and treated fairly through the six-move method.
3. Length fits the target range and the reading level sits at grades 7 to 9 in plain adult language.
4. Every scenario is original, plausible, numerically simple, labeled as illustration, and free of stereotypes, and at least one case shows the obvious recommendation may be wrong.
5. Every Tier B factual claim is researched, dated, and sourced to a primary source in the preferred order, and anything uncertain is marked for review.
6. No individualized advice appears in the body, and the single education-only footer notice is present.
7. Bitcoin language rules are followed wherever Bitcoin appears, and Bitcoin is absent where it would not help.
8. Reentry and youth rules are satisfied where the article will be used with those audiences, including offline-usable exercises and no requests for personal data.
9. Accessibility and mobile standards are met, and the print version stands on its own in black and white with the article ID and revision date in the footer.
10. Design uses only the existing FSA tokens and classes, with no new tokens, no decorative emoji, and no em dashes.
11. Analytics use the first-party FSA service only.
12. The banned phrases are absent, and the voice reads as clear, grounded, and respectful.
13. The editorial state and reviewStatus are set correctly, and both review gates have been passed before approval.

An article that clears this checklist is ready for a human to approve. Nothing skips the checklist, and no article reaches the public in any other way.
