# FSA Originality Standard and Checklist

This document defines what originality means for FSA Money Ideas articles and how to protect it in practice. It applies to every article in the library and works alongside the master FSA Educational Article Standard.

## The goal

The goal is independent FSA work. Every Money Ideas article should be built from FSA's own thinking, framing, and examples, so that the library has a coherent voice and owes its expression to no one else.

This is not about hiding inspiration. Inspiration is normal and healthy. Ideas come from somewhere: a topic surfaces in the FSA curriculum, a question comes up in a workshop, a news event makes a concept suddenly relevant, or a well-made public explainer reminds us that a topic deserves better coverage. Learning from the world is how good educational work happens. The standard is not that FSA must invent concepts from nothing. The standard is that FSA must express those concepts in its own way, and never reproduce another creator's particular expression.

The line to hold is the line between a common concept and borrowed expression. Compounding, opportunity cost, liquidity, and present bias are shared human knowledge, and no one owns them. But the specific way a particular lesson frames compounding, the exact scenario it uses, the analogy it reaches for, the sequence it teaches in, the questions it asks, and the way it summarizes are that creator's expression. FSA teaches the concept in FSA's own expression, always.

## The originality protocol

Follow this protocol for every article. It moves from identifying where the topic came from to building genuinely new FSA material on top of the underlying concept.

**Identify where the topic came from.** Before writing, name the source of the topic honestly to yourself. The source is usually one of: general knowledge, the FSA curriculum, a public educational resource, a news event, a research source, or another article. Naming it is what makes the next steps possible, because you cannot avoid echoing a source you have not consciously identified.

**Extract only the underlying concept.** From the source, take the concept and nothing else. If a public explainer taught you how overdraft fees cascade, extract the mechanism, the plain fact of how the cascade works, and set the explainer aside. Do not carry its wording, its example, its framing, or its structure forward. The concept is shared knowledge. Everything else belongs to the source.

**Create new material from scratch.** Build FSA's own version of each expressive element. Write a new framing question. Name the misconception in FSA's own words. Invent an original scenario with FSA's own numbers and circumstances. Choose FSA's own sequence for teaching the idea. Reach for FSA's own analogy. Design FSA's own learner exercise. Write FSA's own conclusion. Each of these is an expressive choice, and each should be made independently rather than adapted from the source.

**Never reproduce another lesson's expression.** Do not reproduce another lesson's headline, distinctive wording, activity structure, examples, questions, charts, or summaries. This holds even when the other lesson did it well, and especially then, because a strong piece of expression is the most tempting to borrow and the most clearly owned by its creator.

**Distinguish common concepts from borrowed expression.** Keep the distinction active while writing. If you find yourself reusing a phrase, ask whether it is the plain name of a concept or a creator's particular turn of phrase. The plain name is fine. The turn of phrase is not.

**Cite facts, never inspiration.** Cite factual claims to their primary sources, as the evidence standard requires. Do not cite an inspiration as though it were evidence. If a public explainer is where you first learned an idea, that is not a citation, because the explainer is not the evidence. Find the underlying primary source for any factual claim and cite that. Inspiration is not a source line.

**Run a manual similarity review when a specific external lesson inspired the topic.** When a particular external lesson, video, article, or course prompted the article, do a deliberate manual comparison before the article moves forward. Read the finished FSA draft next to the source and check that the framing, misconception, scenario, structure, analogy, exercise, questions, and conclusion are all genuinely FSA's own. Record the result in the originality review note. When the topic came from general knowledge or the FSA curriculum with no single external lesson behind it, note that and no comparison is needed.

## Per-article originality checklist

Run this checklist for every article before it leaves drafting.

- [ ] I have identified where this topic came from and named the source type.
- [ ] I extracted only the underlying concept and set the source's expression aside.
- [ ] The framing question is new and written by FSA.
- [ ] The misconception is stated in FSA's own words.
- [ ] The primary scenario is original to FSA, with FSA's own circumstances and numbers, and labeled as an illustration.
- [ ] The second scenario or boundary case is original to FSA.
- [ ] The mental model is expressed in FSA's own words, not a borrowed slogan.
- [ ] The teaching sequence is FSA's own choice, not copied from the source's order.
- [ ] Any analogy used is FSA's own.
- [ ] The learner exercises (observe today, try it yourself) are original and work with pencil and paper.
- [ ] The question to carry and the conclusion are original and not moralizing.
- [ ] No headline, distinctive phrase, activity structure, example, question, chart, or summary is reproduced from another lesson.
- [ ] Factual claims are cited to primary sources; no inspiration is cited as evidence.
- [ ] If a specific external lesson inspired this article, I ran a manual similarity review and recorded the result.
- [ ] I can distinguish, for every reused word, whether it is a common concept name or borrowed expression, and no borrowed expression remains.

## Originality review note format

Record a short originality review note for each article. Keep it brief and honest. The fields are:

**Concept source.** Where the topic came from: general knowledge, FSA curriculum, a public educational resource, a news event, a research source, or another article. Name the specific source when there is one.

**FSA framing.** One line describing the framing question and misconception FSA chose, in FSA's own words.

**Original scenario.** One line confirming the scenarios are original to FSA and noting what they are about.

**Original activity.** One line confirming the observation exercise and the try-it-yourself exercise are original and offline-usable.

**Potential similarity risk.** An honest note on where the article came closest to a source, or "none identified" if the topic came from general knowledge with no single external lesson behind it.

**Review result.** The outcome: cleared, or the specific changes made to remove an echo, and a note that a manual similarity review was run when a specific external lesson inspired the article.
