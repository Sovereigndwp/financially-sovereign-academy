# FSA Educational Philosophy

The library exists to change how a learner thinks about money, not to grow a list of
rules they follow on trust. Five principles govern every article.

## 1. Teach beneath the rule

A rule is the surface. Underneath every good money rule is a reason, and underneath
the reason are assumptions about someone's situation. Teach the reason and the
assumptions, and the learner can rebuild the rule and see when it breaks.

Worked example. "Build an emergency fund before you invest" is really a claim about
liquidity and timing: investments can drop right when you need cash, borrowing to
cover a shock often costs more than the return you gave up by holding some cash, and
a small buffer stops one surprise from cascading into fees and missed payments. Teach
that, and the learner also sees where it bends. High-interest debt can outrun the
value of a buffer past a point. Very stable income plus cheap credit can justify a
thinner buffer. The rule was never the lesson. The reasoning was.

Practical test: if a learner could only repeat your article's rule but could not
explain why it usually holds or name one case where it does not, the article failed.

## 2. Begin in the learner's world

Start where the learner already lives, in a Friday paycheck, a car repair, a checkout
screen, a bill that arrives at the wrong time. Never open with a definition. A
definition tells the learner what to think before they have any reason to care. A
concrete tension makes them want the idea, and then the idea does real work when it
arrives. Name the technical term only after the learner has felt the thing it points
to, then use the term consistently.

## 3. One article, one idea

Each article carries a single primary concept (the record's `primaryConcept`) and one
misconception it examines. Supporting ideas may appear, but only in service of the one
idea. If a draft is trying to teach two ideas, it is two articles. This is what keeps
the pieces short (900 to 1,400 words, or 650 to 900 for a narrow concept) and keeps
the mental model clean enough to remember.

## 4. Discovery before instruction

Lead the learner to see the idea before you state it. The scenario should make the
point almost land on its own, so that when you name the mental model it confirms
something the learner already half-noticed. The two exercises ("Look for this today"
and "Try it yourself") extend this: they ask the learner to observe and test the idea
in their own life, not to accept it because the article said so. The closing question
is open, never a moral. Discovery respects the learner as a thinker.

## 5. Respect the learner

Financial difficulty is never framed as laziness, ignorance, irresponsibility, lack
of discipline, or moral failure. Many money problems are structural: low or
unpredictable income, thin buffers, systems engineered to make spending frictionless
and saving effortful, fees that fall hardest on people with the least. Name those
constraints honestly. And then, without contradicting them, show where a learner's own
design choices still have leverage. The stance is: the deck is not fair, and you still
have moves. Acknowledge the constraint without removing the agency, and remove the
shame without removing the responsibility. This is not a tone applied on top of the
writing. It is the reason the writing exists.
