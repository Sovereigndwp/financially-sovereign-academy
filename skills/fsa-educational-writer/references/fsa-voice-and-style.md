# FSA Voice and Style

## The voice
FSA writing is clear, thoughtful, grounded, calm, observant, practical, respectful,
and intellectually serious. It treats the reader as a capable adult who has simply not
been shown how a thing works. It is confident without being loud and warm without being
soft.

## What the voice is not
Not preachy. Not childish. Not academic. Not bureaucratic. Not promotional. Not
artificially inspirational. Not template-like. If a sentence sounds like a brochure, a
lecture hall, a compliance memo, or a motivational poster, rewrite it.

## Dignity is part of the voice
Financial difficulty is never framed as laziness, ignorance, irresponsibility, lack of
discipline, or moral failure. Acknowledge structural constraints (low or unpredictable
income, thin buffers, fees, systems built to make spending easy) honestly, and still
leave the reader with agency and moves they can make. Remove the shame without removing
the responsibility.

## Banned phrases
Never use any of these, or close variants:
- "In today's fast-paced world"
- "Let's dive in"
- "The bottom line"
- "It is important to note"
- "Whether you are a beginner or an expert"
- "Empower your financial journey"
- "Unlock" (as in unlock savings, unlock potential)
- "Navigate the complexities"

## Formatting rules that are non-negotiable
- **No em dashes.** Not in article prose and not in this skill's own prose. Use a
  comma, a parenthesis, a colon, or a full stop instead. Restructure the sentence if
  needed.
- **No decorative emoji.** None, anywhere in the body.
- **Use contractions naturally** (it is fine to write "you don't" and "it isn't"). Do
  not force formality.
- **Vary sentence length.** Mix short sentences with longer ones. Do not write chains
  of one-sentence paragraphs; that reads as a slideshow, not an explanation.

## Things to avoid
- Generic motivational endings ("You've got this," "Start your journey today"). The
  article ends on an open question, not a pep talk.
- Excessive rhetorical questions. One or two in a piece is plenty; a run of them feels
  like a sales page.
- One-sentence-paragraph chains. Group related thoughts.
- Artificial transitions ("Now, let's turn to," "With that in mind"). Let ideas follow
  each other on their own logic.
- Over-polished symmetry (every paragraph the same length, every section the same
  shape). Real explanation is a little uneven.
- Long abstract passages with no concrete anchor. Bring an example within a few
  sentences of any abstraction.
- Unexplained jargon. Explain the idea first, then name the term, then reuse it.
- Overuse of "you." Some direct address is good; wall-to-wall "you" starts to nag.

## Sentence rhythm, in practice
Read a draft aloud. If you run out of breath, the sentence is too long or has no
commas doing real work. If every sentence lands with the same beat, break the pattern.
The prototype "Why Saving Money Feels So Hard" is the reference for cadence: plain
words, commas where a lazy writer would reach for a dash, occasional short sentences
for emphasis, no filler.

## A note on the term "present bias" and its kin
When you introduce a technical term (present bias, opportunity cost, liquidity,
compounding), first describe the experience in ordinary words, then say what
researchers call it, then use the term plainly for the rest of the piece. Never lead
with the term. The reader should feel the thing before they get the label.
