# FSA Audience Adaptation Standard

Adaptation changes the examples, the barriers, and the framing so the article speaks to
a specific group. It never changes the core concept, the mental model, or the factual
meaning. A learner in a reentry program and a general adult reader should come away with
the same idea, met where they are.

The registry `audiences` vocabulary is: `general`, `workforce`, `reentry`, `youth`,
`community`, `educators`, `colombia`. The notes below also cover Spanish-speaking US
readers (handled through the Spanish adaptation brief, `templates/spanish-adaptation-brief.md`).

## General adult
The default. Ordinary working life, mixed income levels, no assumed employer or program
context. Examples come from paychecks, bills, stores, and banks that most adults use.

## Workforce
Readers reached through employers or job programs. Examples can lean on paychecks, pay
schedules, direct deposit, and benefits enrollment. Keep it useful to hourly and
shift workers, not just salaried readers. Do not assume a 401(k) or paid leave exists.

## Correctional facility
Assume very limited or no internet, printed delivery, and no smartphone or app access.
No exercise may require an account, a login, or going online. Examples avoid tools the
reader cannot reach. Never ask for criminal history, debt balances, or personal
financial data. Keep numbers simple enough to do with a pencil. This audience overlaps
with reentry but is even more constrained on access.

## Reentry
Readers rebuilding financial life after incarceration or another major disruption.
Assume limited internet and that a bank account may not exist yet. Never require an
active bank account for an exercise, and never ask for criminal history, debt balances,
trauma, or personal financial data. Include realistic barriers where they are relevant:
missing ID, account screening (for example ChexSystems-style denials), unstable
employment, housing deposits, scams that target people in transition, fees, and
transportation. Promise no outcomes and shame no one. Print exercises must work fully
offline.

## Community
Readers reached through libraries, nonprofits, and community organizations. Mixed
circumstances, often shared or group settings. Examples work for a facilitator to use
with a small group. Keep them broadly relatable and low-stakes.

## Youth
Younger learners. Assume no parental financial support and do not ask for household
income. No family-finance spectacle (do not turn a parent's money situation into the
example). Use low-stakes examples appropriate to a young person's own small decisions.
Note where legal rules differ for minors (for example, age limits on accounts,
contracts, or credit). Keep exercises separate from real transactions; a young reader
should be able to think through the idea without being nudged toward buying, signing, or
opening anything.

## Colombia
LATAM-fluent and Colombia-aware. Examples and institutions should reflect Colombian
financial life rather than defaulting to U.S. banks and agencies. Currency, common
account types, and everyday costs should feel local. Factual claims (rates, rules,
programs) must be sourced to Colombian or appropriate regional authorities, not U.S.
ones. This overlaps with, but is not the same as, a Spanish-language version.

## Educators
Readers who will teach the material to others. The adaptation surfaces the reasoning and
the discussion openings, so a teacher can lead a conversation rather than deliver a
conclusion. Facilitator framing and answer guidance matter here.

## Spanish-speaking US
Readers in the United States who prefer Spanish. Localize language and some examples
while keeping U.S. institutions and rules where those are what the reader actually
deals with. Handled through the Spanish adaptation brief after the English article is
approved (see `templates/spanish-adaptation-brief.md`), preserving concept, formality,
sequence, mental model, dignity, and factual meaning.

## What never changes in adaptation
- the primary concept and the misconception it examines,
- the mental model (it may be re-expressed in the target language or register, but it
  means the same thing),
- the factual meaning of any Tier B claim,
- the dignity stance and the neutrality boundary,
- the 13-part structure.

## What may change
- the scenario's setting, character, and everyday details,
- which realistic barriers are named,
- the institutions and currency referenced,
- the reading register (within grade 7 to 9 plain language),
- the specific exercise prompts, as long as they stay offline-friendly and never ask
  for private financial data.
