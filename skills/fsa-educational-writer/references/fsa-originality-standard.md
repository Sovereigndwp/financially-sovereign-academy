# FSA Originality Standard

FSA articles are original work. They explain widely known concepts, so the concepts
are shared, but the framing, the scenarios, the mental-model wording, the exercises,
and the sentence-level prose are the library's own. Copied or lightly paraphrased text
from another source is never acceptable, even when the source is accurate.

## The originality protocol
1. **Separate the concept from the expression.** The concept (opportunity cost,
   present bias) belongs to everyone. How you introduce it, the example you build, and
   the line you compress it into are yours to create.
2. **Build the scenario from scratch.** Do not reuse a well-known teaching example
   (the "latte factor," a textbook's exact numbers, a famous marshmallow study setup
   presented as your own). Invent an ordinary situation with its own name and numbers.
3. **Write the mental model in FSA's own words.** It should not match a known slogan or
   a competitor's tagline.
4. **Design original exercises.** The observation and try-it-yourself activities are
   created for the article, not lifted from a worksheet.
5. **Check for accidental similarity.** If a phrase is memorable, ask whether it is
   memorable because it is already someone else's. When in doubt, rewrite it.
6. **Cite the underlying source for facts, not the phrasing.** Tier B facts get a
   citation (see `references/fsa-source-standard.md`); the sentences around them are
   still original.

## The originality review note
Every article carries a short originality note, produced at review time and recorded
with the draft. Use exactly these fields:

- **Concept source:** where the underlying idea comes from (established behavioral
  economics, general personal-finance knowledge, a named study for a Tier B claim).
- **FSA framing:** what is original about how this article approaches the idea.
- **Original scenario:** confirmation that the scenario, its character, and its numbers
  were created for this article and are labeled as an FSA illustration.
- **Original activity:** confirmation that the two exercises were designed for this
  article.
- **Potential similarity risk:** any phrase, example, or structure that might resemble
  a known source, and what was done about it (rewritten, or judged safe and why).
- **Review result:** a one-line judgment, for example "Original; no similarity risk
  found" or "Original after rewriting the opening metaphor."

The originality note is descriptive, not decorative. If it cannot honestly say the work
is original, that is a finding for the human reviewer, not something to smooth over.
