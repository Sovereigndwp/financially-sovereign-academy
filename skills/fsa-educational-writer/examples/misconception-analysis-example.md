# Example: a worked misconception using the six-step method

An illustration of the misconception method, not paste-ready text. The concept here is
"free," chosen to differ from the prototype. Build your own analysis for each article.

**Misconception under examination:** "Free means it costs you nothing."

## 1. Why the belief seems reasonable
No money leaves your pocket. A free checking account, a free trial, a free phone with a
plan: at the moment you say yes, your balance does not change. People are right that the
cash cost is zero at that instant, and it is sensible to notice when cash is not leaving.

## 2. What it gets right
The belief correctly tracks one real thing: the immediate cash price. And plenty of
free things are genuinely worth taking. Skepticism about the word "free" should not curdle
into refusing every no-cost offer. Sometimes free is just free.

## 3. What it misses
Price is not the same as cost. When cash does not change hands, the cost usually moved
somewhere else: to your time (a free trial you forget to cancel), to your attention and
data (a free app that sells ad targeting), to a later bill (free financing that charges
back-interest if you miss the window), or to a different person entirely. The cost did
not vanish. It changed shape, timing, or owner.

## 4. A better model
Free usually changes who pays, when they pay, or what they give up. So the useful move is
not "avoid free," it is "find where the cost went."

## 5. When the better model is useful
Any time the word "free" is doing persuasive work: free trial, free checking, free
financing, free shipping over a threshold, a free gift for signing up. Asking "where did
the cost go?" turns a reflex yes into a real decision.

## 6. Where even the better model has limits
Some things really are free to you with no hidden catch, a public library card, a
government form, a friend's genuine favor. Treating every free thing as a trap is its own
error and can cost a person real benefits they were entitled to. The model is a question
to ask, not a verdict to assume.

## What to copy from this (the technique, not the words)
- Take the belief seriously before complicating it (steps 1 and 2).
- Locate the exact thing it misses (step 3), then compress the fix into a model (step 4).
- Bound the model on both sides: when it helps (step 5) and where it overreaches (step 6).
- Keep the tone fair to the person who held the belief. No shaming, no em dashes, no emoji.
