# Example: an original, numerically simple scenario

An illustration of scenario craft, not text to reuse. Concept: price vs cost (why the
lower price can be the more expensive choice). Every article needs its own scenario with
its own character and numbers.

## The scenario
> Rosa needs work boots. The store has two pairs. One is thirty dollars and the other is
> ninety. The thirty-dollar pair looks fine, so she buys it. Four months later the sole
> splits and she buys the same cheap pair again. By the end of the year she has bought
> three pairs and spent ninety dollars, the same as the good pair, except her feet hurt
> for months and she lost an afternoon each time replacing them.
>
> The ninety-dollar boots would have lasted two years. Over that stretch the "expensive"
> pair was the cheaper choice, and the "cheap" pair carried the higher cost.

## Why it works as an FSA scenario
- **Ordinary life.** Boots, a store, a budget. Nothing exotic.
- **Numerically simple.** Three numbers: 30, 90, and a count of purchases. The reader
  does the arithmetic in their head and the point lands.
- **The concept is almost visible on its own.** The gap between price (30) and cost
  (90 plus sore feet plus lost afternoons) shows up before you name it.
- **It has a plain-named character.** Rosa, not "a consumer."
- **It is honest, not rigged.** The cheap pair really did have the lower price. The
  scenario does not pretend the expensive option is always right; that nuance is for the
  boundary case.

## Labeling
In the source file, this goes under "Illustrative examples created by FSA": Rosa and the
boots are invented for teaching, the numbers are rounded, and it is not a data point.

## What to copy from this (the technique, not the words)
- Pick numbers a person can hold in their head, and make them do the teaching.
- Let the concept emerge from the situation instead of stating it.
- Give the character a name and an ordinary need.
- Keep the scenario fair to the choice the reader would actually make.
- No em dashes, no emoji.
