# Example: a boundary case where the obvious recommendation is not clearly right

An illustration of the boundary-case technique, not reusable text. Concept: emergency
funds (the common "build an emergency fund before anything else" advice). Every article
needs its own boundary case built from its own concept.

## The obvious recommendation
"Everyone should build a cash emergency fund before doing anything else with their money."
It is common advice, and for many people it is good advice. The boundary case does not
attack it. It shows where it stops being obviously right.

## The boundary case
> Consider someone carrying a credit-card balance at a high interest rate while trying to
> build cash savings in an account that pays very little. Every month, the interest piling
> up on the debt can outrun what the savings are earning. For this person, parking a large
> pile of cash at a low rate while the debt grows faster can leave them worse off than if
> they had put some of that money toward the balance first. The "obvious" rule to save
> before all else may not be the right call for them.
>
> This does not flip into a new universal rule ("always pay debt first"). A person with no
> buffer at all can be one flat tire away from more high-interest debt, so some cash on
> hand still matters. The honest answer is: it depends on the actual interest rates and how
> thin the buffer is. Check the numbers in front of you before following any general rule,
> including this one.

## Why it works
- **It names a real case where the obvious advice can be wrong**, which the structure
  requires.
- **It refuses to become a new rigid rule.** It replaces one rule with a question, not
  with the opposite rule.
- **It sends the learner to the numbers**, which is the point of teaching beneath the rule.
- **It keeps dignity.** No one is called foolish for following common advice; the situation,
  not the person, is what changes the answer.

## What to copy from this (the technique, not the words)
- Start from the obvious recommendation and grant that it is often right.
- Find the situation where it quietly fails.
- Resist inventing the mirror-image rule; hand the learner a check-the-numbers question.
- Protect the reader's dignity throughout.
- No em dashes, no emoji.
