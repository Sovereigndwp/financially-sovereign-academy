# FSA Money Ideas: Gold Standard Rubric (v1, from the Pilot Collection)

This is the benchmark every future Money Ideas article is measured against. It is
derived from the two Pilot Collection articles that cleared the full six-stage pipeline:

- **FSA-ARTICLE-000** "The Most Expensive Word in Personal Finance: 'Later'" (the library intro)
- **FSA-ARTICLE-001** "Why Saving Money Feels So Hard" (the single-concept exemplar)

It does not replace `skills/fsa-educational-writer/references/fsa-educational-review-rubric.md`
(the ten scoring dimensions). It makes that rubric concrete: for each dimension it records
what a 5 actually looked like in the pilot, so a reviewer can compare rather than guess.
Status: v1, pending Dalia's calibration review. Update it after each series retrospective.

## How to use it
1. Draft to the 13-part structure and FSA voice as usual.
2. Score the draft 1 to 5 on the ten dimensions. Any dimension below 4 blocks approval.
3. Compare each dimension against the "what a 5 looked like" anchor below. If the draft is
   not clearly at that bar, it is not yet gold standard.
4. Run the writing-audit checklist (em dashes, emoji, banned phrases, rhetorical-question
   count, hidden access assumptions). Any problem is a finding to fix.
5. The writer never self-approves. Hand off at `drafted` / `human-review-required`.

## The ten dimensions, with pilot anchors

1. **Concept accuracy (anchor: 5).** The term is used correctly and never overclaimed. In
   "Later," present bias and procrastination are named only after the feeling is described,
   and compounding is stated as a direction ("changes the size of the number, not its date"),
   not a promised figure. Gold standard = a specialist would not wince.

2. **Learner clarity (anchor: 5, watch the density).** Grade 7 to 9, mechanism before label.
   "Why Saving" is the cadence reference. The one place the pilot flirted with abstraction was
   the compounding paragraph in "Later"; gold standard keeps at most one dense passage per
   article, and flags it as a candidate for a companion visual rather than making the prose
   carry it alone.

3. **Practical relevance (anchor: 5).** The reader could use it this week. "Later" opens on
   decisions the reader is postponing right now; "Why Saving" opens on a Friday paycheck.

4. **Mental-model strength (anchor: 5).** One line that survives outside the article.
   "Today competes with tomorrow." "'Later' borrows from your future self." Test: it applies to
   a decision the article never mentions.

5. **Scenario quality (anchor: 5).** One ordinary situation, one or two figures, a plain name,
   labeled an FSA illustration in the source file. Gold standard is Rosa's single $12 charge
   (8 x $12 = $96) and Marcus's $40: the reader does the arithmetic in their head.

6. **Nuance (anchor: 5).** The boundary case must hold two truths at once without contradiction
   and must include a case where the obvious move is wrong for someone. Both pilots do this: for
   a person with no slack, "act now / save first" can be the wrong instruction, and the article
   says so plainly and frames it as "check the numbers in front of you."

7. **Evidence quality (anchor: 5).** Tier A stable concepts rest on named peer-reviewed sources;
   Tier B current facts are dated or absent; FSA scenarios are quarantined as illustration.
   Gold standard, shown by both pilots: prefer articles that carry **no** dated Tier B claim, so
   nothing goes stale. Nothing is ever invented; a missing source is a blocker, not a guess.

8. **Tone and dignity (anchor: 5).** Names structural constraints (low or unpredictable income,
   thin buffers, systems built to make spending easy), removes shame, keeps agency. No moralizing.

9. **Originality (anchor: 5).** Framing, scenario, model wording, and exercises are the library's
   own. The pilot avoided the "latte factor" and every stock example; the voting-for-the-default
   image and Rosa are original. The originality note must honestly say so.

10. **FSA integration (anchor: 5).** Correct series, canonical module names, real related slugs,
    the education-only footer, `noindex` until approved, and all 13 parts present and in order.

## Writing-audit checklist (must all pass)
No em dashes anywhere. No decorative emoji. No banned phrases. At most one or two rhetorical
questions in the prose (exercise and closing questions are structural parts, not counted). No
hidden assumptions about a bank account, smartphone, spare cash, or internet in the exercises.
Varied paragraph and sentence length. Term explained before it is named. No copied phrasing.
