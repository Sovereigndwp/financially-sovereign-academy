## FSA-ARTICLE-000: "The Most Expensive Word in Personal Finance: 'Later'"

### A1. Time preference is the article's thesis and is never named. **Recommended.**

**Concept:** time preference (Böhm-Bawerk).
**Status:** load-bearing but unnamed.

The article explains present bias carefully:

> "When the effort is now and the payoff is later, we put a heavy thumb on the scale for right
> now. Researchers call this present bias."

Present bias is a **deviation**. The article never establishes what it is a deviation from.
Every person trades present goods against future goods at some rate, and that rate is not a
defect. It is a structural feature of choosing under time, and it differs legitimately between
people and between months.

**Why this strengthens the article's own argument, and is not merely technical.** The boundary
case is where FSA's dignity rule lives, and it currently asserts its point:

> "If the money to act is not there this week, being told to act immediately misses the real
> constraint, which is room, not character."

With the baseline established, that sentence stops being an assertion and becomes a
consequence. A person whose present needs are unmet **rationally** values present goods more
highly. Their time preference is high because their situation is urgent, not because their
character is weak. The article currently says "not character" and hopes the reader believes
it. Naming the baseline lets the article show why.

**Proposed change:** one clause where present bias is introduced, along the lines of
*"Everyone weighs the present against the future at some rate, and the rate moves with your
circumstances. Present bias is what we call it when that weighting runs ahead of your own
considered ranking."* Term after mechanism, per the voice standard.

### A2. Interest is the price of time, not a fee. **Recorded, not recommended.**

> "interest compounds, so what you owe grows on top of what already grew"

Correct. An Austrian reading would add that interest exists because present money is worth
more than future money to nearly everyone, so the lender is compensated for waiting. True, and
it connects neatly to A1.

**Not recommended** because the article is teaching a household to notice a growing balance,
and the origin of interest does not change what they should notice this month. It belongs in
a future article on where interest comes from, which already exists in the registry as a
planned record.

### A3. "Later votes for the default" is doing correct economics. **Correct, no change.**

Not acting is itself an action with a cost, which is precisely the point. The article gets
this right and phrases it better than the technical vocabulary would.

### Rejected as out of scope (000)

- **Monetary debasement as the reason saving feels futile.** A reader deciding whether to
  cancel a twelve dollar subscription is not helped by this. Fails the governing test and the
  scale rule.

## FSA-ARTICLE-001: "Why Saving Money Feels So Hard"

### B1. Same unnamed baseline as A1. **Recommended, and cheaper here.**

**Concept:** time preference.
**Status:** load-bearing but unnamed.

The mental model is already an unnamed statement of it:

> "Today competes with tomorrow, over and over, and the setup decides who tends to win."

That is a good line and it should not change. What is missing is a single sentence
establishing that the competition is normal and that the rate at which a person resolves it
moves with their circumstances. Because 001 already devotes a full boundary section to the
low-income case, the payoff is larger here than in 000 for the same one sentence.

**Note for sequencing:** if both 000 and 001 name it, 000 should introduce it (it is the
library intro) and 001 should reuse it. That is a question for the calibration sitting, not
for this reviewer.

### B2. The friction asymmetry is stated as design without a mechanism, and reads as intent. **Recommended.**

> "Spending has been engineered to be smooth. Cards are saved, checkout is one tap, and money
> leaves without ceremony."

The observation is right and the mechanism is missing, and the gap has a cost: "engineered"
implies somebody engineered it *at you*. The plainer mechanism is competitive. A firm that
removes a step from checkout completes more sales than one that does not, so over time every
surviving checkout is smooth. Nobody had to intend the asymmetry for it to appear everywhere.
Saving faces no such competition because no one profits from the step you did not take.

**Why this strengthens the article's own argument.** It converts a claim the reader might
dismiss as cynical into one they can verify from ordinary experience, and it is more useful:
it predicts where else the reader will meet the same asymmetry. It also protects the FSA voice
standard, which bans preachiness. Attributing intent to firms is the closest this article gets
to it.

### B3. The debt-versus-savings comparison. **Correct, one gap.**

> "money sitting in a low-interest savings account can lose ground to the interest piling up
> on what you owe"

Correct, and correctly hedged. The gap: money in a savings account can lose ground to rising
prices even when there is no debt at all. This is not an Austrian point, it is universal, and
its absence in a savings article is a genuine hole.

**Proposed change is a link, not a claim.** `FSA-ARTICLE-010`
("How Inflation Changes Your Choices") already exists in the registry as a planned record. Add
it to related articles when it is written. **No new claim, no new sourcing burden, no dated
figure.** Recommended as a registry note rather than body text.

### Rejected as out of scope (001)

- **Reframing low savings rates as a consequence of monetary policy.** Policy. Also a dignity
  violation: it tells a reader whose problem is a $40 transfer that their problem is somewhere
  else entirely.
- **"Saving is deferred consumption that funds capital formation."** True and macro. A
  household deciding about Friday's forty dollars is not the unit of analysis.

## FSA-ARTICLE-002: "Every Dollar Has Four Jobs"

### C1. Opportunity cost is stated objectively when the article needs it subjective. **Recommended. Strongest finding in the run.**

**Concept:** opportunity cost as subjective and forward-looking (Wieser; Buchanan, *Cost and
Choice*, 1969).
**Status:** imprecise, and the imprecision undercuts the article's own boundary case.

> "Economists call the value of the best declined option opportunity cost, and it is running in
> your budget whether you look at it or not."

As written this reads as though the cost is an objective quantity sitting in the budget,
computable by anyone with the numbers. It is not. Opportunity cost exists **in the mind of the
chooser at the moment of choice**, and it is the value that person places on what they gave
up. Two people with identical balance sheets can face different opportunity costs for the same
dollar, and neither is wrong.

**Why this is the strongest finding.** The article's boundary case already asserts exactly
this, and asserts it without support:

> "Check the actual rates, the actual cushion, and the actual month before following anyone's
> ranking of the jobs, including this article's."

That instruction is currently a modest disclaimer. If cost is subjective, it becomes a
**conclusion the article has earned**: no external ranking of the four jobs can be right for
you, because the cost of each declined job is something only you can value. The article
already argues that the neighbor sending the same forty dollars to the card "might be just as
right." Subjective cost is *why*.

**Proposed change:** adjust the definition so it locates the cost in the chooser.
Something in the shape of *"the value you place on the best thing you turned down"*, and
"running in your budget" becomes "running behind every choice you make, whether you look at it
or not." Two clauses. No new term, no source change, no citation needed beyond what the source
file already carries.

### C2. The exercise ranks; say so. **Recommended, small.**

> "Next to each, one word for how your ordinary month treats it: crowded, thin, or empty. No
> dollar figures, nothing to show anyone."

This is deliberately ordinal, and the design is correct: people rank, they do not measure
utility. One clause making it explicit that ranking is the point, rather than a simplification
for the reader's convenience, would stop a numerate reader from assuming the exercise is a
watered-down budget. The article's own line, *"The column is a picture of where your money is
pointed, not a grade,"* is already halfway there.

### C3. Grow's mechanism is asserted. **Recorded, not recommended for this article.**

> "buy a piece of the future through investing, ownership, or learning that raises what you can
> earn"

Correct. The mechanism underneath is the time structure of capital: more roundabout methods of
production yield more, and take longer, which is why Grow pays and why it pays later. That is
a real explanation and it is the right depth for an investing article, not for a 1,450-word
piece whose job is to get one free dollar assigned. Recorded for the Financial Independence
series.

### C4. The four jobs are categories; choice happens at the margin. **Correct, no change.**

The article already gets the hardest part right. Its whole move is from "split my money four
ways" (categorical) to "which job does the **next free dollar** take" (marginal). That is
correct marginal analysis, arrived at without the vocabulary, and it should stay without the
vocabulary.

### Conflict check

None. The Federal Reserve Education citation for opportunity cost is appropriate, supports the
claim it is attached to, and stays. The reviewer has no provenance objection and is not
permitted one.
