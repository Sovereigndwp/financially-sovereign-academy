## Editorial brief

**What the learner may believe.** That handling money well means dividing it up among the
grown-up jobs: save some, pay down debt, put some toward the future. The unspoken
assumption is that there are dollars left over to divide, and that a person who is not
doing all of these is doing something wrong.

**What to notice (the shift in attention).** Every dollar can do only one job at a time,
and for many households most dollars are already claimed by the first job, covering what
must be paid now. The useful question is not "how do I split my money four ways?" It is
"which job is each dollar already doing, and when a free dollar shows up, which job does it
need most?" That reframe moves the reader from feeling behind to making one clear decision
at the margin.

**The four jobs (as this article will name them).** A dollar can: **cover now** (needs and
obligations that are due), **wait** (a buffer for near-term surprises and goals), **repay**
(shrink debt already owed), or **grow** (buy a share of future productivity through
investing or ownership). One dollar, one job at a time.

**Why it matters.** Seeing money as jobs rather than a pile to divide changes the decision
from a vague "I should be doing more" into a concrete "this dollar is doing X; my next free
dollar would do the most good doing Y." It also removes a quiet source of shame: a person
whose income is fully spoken for by the first job is not failing at budgeting. They are
facing a math and structure problem, not a discipline problem, and naming that honestly is
part of the lesson.

**Realistic scenario (to build in the draft, Tier C illustration).** A household whose
paycheck is almost fully committed: rent, groceries, transport to work, and the minimum on
one debt. After those, very little or nothing is left. For most of the month, the "four
jobs" question has one honest answer, cover now, and that is not a personal failure. Then
one month a recurring cost ends (a phone paid off, a season pass expires) and forty dollars
comes free. The article follows that one free dollar and asks which single job it should
take. Round numbers only; the point is the decision, not the arithmetic.

**Main factual claims needing evidence.** The concepts (money allocation, opportunity cost,
liquidity) are Tier A and rest on established ideas. There is **one optional Tier B claim**
the draft may reach for: a statistic about how many households cannot easily cover a small
emergency expense. If used, it must be sourced to a primary source (for example the Federal
Reserve's Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking) and dated. If it cannot be
sourced cleanly, the draft should make the point through the scenario instead and cite
nothing it cannot stand behind.

**Learner action (to design in the draft).** An observation exercise: notice one dollar you
spent today and name which of the four jobs it did. A try-it exercise, pencil and paper and
fully offline: if one free dollar (or ten, or forty) appeared this week, write which single
job it would do and one sentence on why, with no requirement to have the money or to reveal
any real balance.

**Ending question (open, not moralizing).** If most of your dollars already have a job, what
would have to change for a few of them to get a different one?

**What the article will deliberately NOT cover.** How to build a full budget, which specific
account or investment to use, how to get out of debt, or any individualized recommendation.
Those belong to other Money Ideas articles and to the Mindset & Cash Flow, Debt, Emergency
Funds, and Investing modules. This article teaches one idea: a dollar does one job, and the
real skill is choosing the job for the next free one.

## Misconception analysis (six-step method)

1. **Why the belief seems reasonable.** Common advice really does say to save, pay down debt,
   and invest, and doing all three is genuinely good when it is possible. It is sensible to
   want to be responsible on every front.
2. **What it gets right.** Money does have distinct jobs, and being deliberate about which
   dollar does which is a real and useful skill. Giving a dollar a purpose beats letting it
   drift.
3. **What it misses.** It quietly assumes there are spare dollars to divide. For many
   households most dollars are already claimed by the first job, covering what is due now.
   Telling that person to "split their money four ways" misreads the situation as a discipline
   gap when it is a math and structure reality.
4. **A better model.** A dollar holds one job at a time, and for many people most dollars
   already have one. The skill is not dividing a surplus that is not there; it is naming what
   each dollar is doing and choosing the job for the next free dollar, if and when one appears.
5. **When the better model is useful.** Any time a person feels behind for not saving or
   investing while their income is fully committed, and any time a small surplus does appear
   and the question becomes where it should go.
6. **Where even the better model has limits.** "One dollar, one job" is a lens, not a ledger.
   Real money is fungible and priorities shift within a month. And the model does not by
   itself say which job wins; that depends on the person's debt rates, buffer, and
   circumstances, which is the boundary case, not a formula.

## Mental model

**"A dollar holds one job at a time, and for many people most dollars already have one."**
Reusable, honest about constrained budgets, and it sets up the real decision: which job does
the next free dollar need most.

## Scenario proposal

The committed-paycheck household above (needs and one minimum debt payment consume almost
everything), then one recurring cost ends and a small, round amount comes free. Follow that
single free dollar through the four jobs. It is ordinary, numerically simple, respectful, and
it makes the concept visible without pretending four equal choices exist.

## Boundary case

Where the "obvious" move flips, presented as "check the numbers," never as a rule:

- If there is **no surplus at all**, the four-jobs question mostly does not apply yet, and
  that is about income and structure, not character. The honest move may be timing bills
  against pay, not choosing among four jobs.
- If a **high-interest debt** is growing, sending the free dollar to **repay** can beat
  parking it in low-interest savings.
- If there is a **workplace match** on retirement contributions, **grow** can beat repaying a
  low-interest debt, because the match is a large, immediate gain.
- If there is **no buffer at all**, a small **wait** (a first cushion) can beat both repaying
  and growing, because one surprise could otherwise create new high-interest debt.

The article must include at least the no-surplus case and one case where the tidy "invest for
the future" or "always pay debt first" advice is not clearly right.

## Originality plan

- **Concept source:** general personal-finance knowledge about allocating money by purpose;
  the "jobs for money" framing is widespread.
- **Similarity risk (flag for human review).** The phrase **"give every dollar a job"** is a
  distinctive, well-known slogan associated with the budgeting product YNAB (You Need A
  Budget), and this article's working title is close to that territory. This is a real
  originality risk. FSA's angle must be clearly its own: not "assign every dollar" (a
  budgeting instruction) but "most dollars are already assigned, so decide the next free one"
  (a reasoning move), with an original scenario, an original mental-model sentence, and
  original exercises. A manual similarity review against YNAB's language is required before
  this article advances, and the title may need adjustment.
- **FSA framing to keep original:** the committed-paycheck starting point, the "next free
  dollar" decision, the refusal to assume four equal choices, and the dignity stance.
- **Original scenario and activities:** the freed-forty-dollars household and both exercises
  are to be created fresh for this article.
